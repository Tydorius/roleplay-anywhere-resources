STOREYS (the floors extension)
- A building entry may add "storeys": 1-3 — up to 6 when it has "units" or is
  a part of a compound. Use upper floors where the setting has them: inns,
  town houses, apartment stacks, towers, a ship's decks. Most buildings stay
  single-storey (omit the field, or use 1).
- A unit building's "units" may add "perStorey" (units per floor; omit it to
  spread the count over the storeys): {"name": "Cabin", "count": 9,
  "perStorey": 3, "residents": 2} with "storeys": 3 fills three decks.
- "numbering": "plain" names the copies Apartment 1, 2, 3 across the floors;
  "storey" numbers each floor's rooms 101, 102 ... 201, 202 (hotel or
  hospital style).
- A lodging's room count fits its settlement: about 2 rooms for a hamlet,
  4 for a village, 10 for a town, 24 for a city. An inn in a hamlet is a
  common hall and two rooms, not a hotel.
- Bunks and barracks are one dormitory room, not units: give the building
  "residents" (one per bunk) and let one big room hold the beds.
- Building heights fit the era and its settlements (the code lowers anything
  over these): medieval — hamlets and villages single-storey (the inn, tavern,
  barn, byre, mill, granary, manor house, temple or shrine hall, or the
  headman's, elder's, chief's or reeve's house may rise to two), towns two,
  cities three (merchant houses, guildhalls and townhouses three in cities;
  cathedrals and great temples three; castles, keeps, towers and fortresses
  four anywhere); industrial — villages two, towns three, cities four
  (tenements, mills, factories and warehouses four; farmhouses and cottages
  two); modern — villages two, towns three, cities six (apartment blocks,
  offices, hotels and hospitals six; houses two; sheds, barns and garages
  one); science fiction — three decks everywhere (stations, ships and
  habitats up to six). A barn's upper storey is a hayloft: hay storage, never
  beds.
